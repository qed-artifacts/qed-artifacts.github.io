House of Quality

Article revision revision.dictionary.2b12b7c084fed54c8d034160

A House of Quality (HoQ) is a planning matrix that relates customer requirements to proposed design or service characteristics, recording a team's assessment of how those characteristics could address the requirements to inform priorities and targets (Martí Bigorra & Isaksson, 2017; Maritan, 2014). It is a tool commonly used in quality function deployment (QFD), but the matrix alone is not the whole QFD process (Hauser & Clausing, 1988; Maritan, 2014; Mazur, 2008).

How the matrix works

Customer requirements occupy one axis and proposed design or service characteristics the other; the intersecting cells record the team's assessment of how strongly each characteristic addresses each requirement (Maritan, 2014; Martí Bigorra & Isaksson, 2017). Teams can use those relationships with customer priorities and other planning information to choose which responses to develop and what targets to propose (Martí Bigorra & Isaksson, 2017; Maritan, 2014).

Common additions

In a familiar house-shaped layout, the roof relates technical characteristics to one another, whereas the main body relates those characteristics to customer requirements (Maritan, 2014; Zare Mehrjerdi, 2010). Importance ratings, competitive comparisons, and target values may be added; their arrangement and calculation depend on the method being used (Martí Bigorra & Isaksson, 2017; Maritan, 2014).

Distinguish from related terms

QFD is the broader work of connecting customer needs to design and subsequent decisions; a House of Quality is one matrix used in many QFD approaches, and some approaches do not use it (Maritan, 2014; Mazur, 2008). A relationship recorded in the matrix is a planning assessment of a proposed design response, not a measurement that the finished offering meets the requirement (Maritan, 2014; Martí Bigorra & Isaksson, 2017).

Constructed example

A bus-shelter team puts riders' need to stay dry in wind along one axis and proposed overhang depth, side-panel angle, and drainage capacity along the other. The team assesses which characteristics address the need, identifies tensions among them, and considers design targets. A table that only lists the need beside the features, without assessed relationships, is a constructed nonexample.

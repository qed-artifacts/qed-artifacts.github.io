specified acceptance decision

Acceptance criterion

An acceptance criterion is a condition or decision rule used to determine whether a specified item, result, or process may be accepted for a stated purpose.

Read explanation

It can specify a limit, an observable condition, or another checkable basis for the decision. State what is being accepted, how the condition is checked, and who may decide.

A requirement may need one or more acceptance criteria to make its fulfillment assessable. Passing one criterion does not by itself establish the object’s overall quality.

sample-based lot acceptance decision

Acceptance sampling

Acceptance sampling is a method for deciding whether to accept or reject a lot by inspecting a sample under a specified decision rule.

Read explanation

The plan identifies the lot, how units are selected, the sample size, what counts as nonconforming, and the number or pattern of findings that triggers rejection.

A sample decision does not establish the condition of every unit. Any plan carries a chance of accepting a poor lot or rejecting a good one, so its risks must fit the intended use.

automotive-oriented product and production-process planning

Advanced product quality planning (APQP)

Advanced product quality planning (APQP) is an automotive-oriented approach for coordinating how a product and its production process will meet customer requirements.

Read explanation

It links planning, design, risk analysis, process preparation, and evidence of readiness before launch. Methods such as failure mode and effects analysis, measurement system analysis, and production part approval can contribute to that work.

APQP is a planning framework, not a single test or a synonym for product quality. Its particular deliverables depend on the applicable customer and industry requirements.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

quality-related spending to evaluate conformity or performance

Appraisal cost

An appraisal cost is a cost incurred to measure, inspect, test, or audit whether products, services, or processes meet relevant quality requirements.

Read explanation

It pays for obtaining and evaluating evidence of conformity, such as a product inspection or process audit. Finding a defect is not the same as preventing it.

In cost-of-quality accounting, appraisal is distinct from prevention and from the costs of correcting failures. Where one activity serves several purposes, the accounting boundary should be stated.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

purpose-bound examination

Assessment

Assessment is the process of gathering and interpreting information about a specified subject for a stated question or decision.

Read explanation

An assessment may compare evidence with requirements, criteria, or a reference, then report findings or a judgment. Its methods and evidential demands depend on its purpose.

The term is used differently across fields. It often overlaps with evaluation; an assessment need not always conclude with an overall appraisal of merit or worth.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

descriptive property of an object

Attribute

An attribute is a feature or property ascribed to, observed in, or recorded for an object.

Read explanation

An attribute can be described by a category, a number, or words. Its recorded state is different from the attribute itself.

The label alone does not show that the feature is inherent, quality-relevant, or favorable. State the object and the role the attribute plays in the assessment.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

result of evidence evaluation against audit criteria

Audit finding

An audit finding is a result of comparing collected audit evidence with the audit criteria.

Read explanation

A finding can record that a requirement is met or identify a gap; it is not synonymous with a defect.

Findings inform the audit conclusion, which considers the audit’s objectives and the findings together.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

evaluation comparison reference

Benchmark

A benchmark is a reference for comparison.

Read explanation

In evaluation, it helps answer “How does this result compare with what a relevant peer has achieved, or with what appears achievable under similar conditions?”

A relevant comparison may use recent performance by similar organizations or a reasoned estimate of what is achievable under similar conditions.

In some uses, a benchmark specifically represents leading practice or performance.

Thus, a benchmark need not mean “the best” in every field.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

metrological relation between reference values and indications

Calibration

Calibration is an operation that establishes how a measuring instrument’s indications relate to reference quantity values under stated conditions, accounting for their uncertainties.

Read explanation

That relation is then used to obtain a measurement result from an indication. A useful calibration record identifies the references, conditions, results, and uncertainties relevant to its use.

Calibration does not necessarily adjust the instrument or make every later result accurate. Adjustment is a separate action, and changes in conditions or the instrument can affect the relation.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

governed change to a controlled product, process or system

Change control

Change control is a process for identifying a proposed change, assessing its effects, authorizing it, and recording what was done and the resulting state.

Read explanation

The depth of review should fit the change and its potential effects on quality, safety, compliance, and operation. Implementation may require updated procedures, records, or renewed validation.

A record that something changed is not enough: control also requires a decision about whether and how the change may proceed.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

proposal initiating formal change control

Change request

In change control, a change request is a documented proposal to alter a controlled product, process, document, or system, submitted for evaluation before implementation.

Read explanation

It identifies the proposed change, why it is sought, and what may be affected. Reviewers then assess its effects and decide whether to authorize it.

Submitting a request starts the review; it does not itself permit the change to be made.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

descriptive feature of an object

Characteristic

A characteristic is a distinguishable feature by which an object can be described.

Read explanation

It may be observed directly, inferred, or assigned in a description. Naming a characteristic does not say whether it is desirable.

To use one in a quality judgment, identify the object and explain how that feature bears on an applicable requirement or other stated basis.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

assertion offered for assessment

Claim

A claim is a statement put forward for acceptance that can be supported, qualified, or challenged.

Read explanation

Claims can concern facts, values, or proposed actions. State the specific assertion and its scope so the evidence needed to assess it can be identified.

A claim is distinct from the evidence offered for it. Finding similar words in a source does not show that the source supports the same assertion.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

routine variation inherent in a process as it currently operates

Common-cause variation

Common-cause variation is the ongoing variation produced by the recurring influences within a process as it currently operates.

Read explanation

When those influences remain sufficiently stable, results vary within a pattern that can be estimated from process data. A single high or low result within that pattern does not by itself identify a new cause.

Reducing this variation generally requires changing the process or its conditions, rather than reacting separately to each observation.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

comparison with alternatives in a House of Quality

Competitive assessment

In a House of Quality, competitive assessment compares an offering with relevant alternatives on customer requirements, technical characteristics, or both.

Read explanation

A customer-side comparison asks how alternatives satisfy the needs customers care about. A technical-side comparison examines the corresponding design characteristics; the two comparisons should be kept distinct.

The result depends on which alternatives, evidence, and scales are used. It can inform priorities or targets, but it does not by itself determine the best design.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

aggregate-index and constitutive-component technical uses

Composite indicator

In one common use, a composite indicator is a single index formed by combining several indicators according to a stated rule.

Read explanation

The choice of components, scales, weights, and combination rule affects what the index represents and how cases rank. Its score should be interpreted with those choices in view.

In some measurement models, the same term instead names an item that helps constitute the concept being indexed, rather than merely reflecting it. State which sense is intended.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

meeting stated requirements

Conformance

Conformance is the state or degree to which an object or activity meets applicable requirements or a stated specification.

Read explanation

A conformance claim needs to identify the object, the requirements checked, and the evidence used. Meeting a specification establishes only what that specification covers; it may leave a user’s other needs unaddressed.

Conformance and conformity are often used interchangeably. Neither word, by itself, names a distinct kind of quality.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

fulfillment of an applicable requirement

Conformity

Conformity is the condition of meeting an applicable requirement.

Read explanation

A claim of conformity should say which object and requirement are involved. When several requirements apply, identify the set covered by the claim and how fulfillment was established.

Conformity to a requirement does not guarantee suitability for every use. In many contexts, conformance names the same relationship.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

determining whether relevant requirements are fulfilled

Conformity assessment

Conformity assessment is the determination of whether an object fulfills relevant specified requirements.

Read explanation

The object, requirements, assessment method, and scope must be clear. Depending on the case, assessment may use testing, inspection, or certification.

A conformity result addresses the requirements actually assessed; it does not, by itself, establish overall excellence or fitness for every use.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

effect of an event or action on an objective or interest

Consequence

A consequence is an effect that follows from an event, action, or decision and matters to an objective or interest.

Read explanation

A decision may have several consequences, favorable or harmful, immediate or delayed. Specify the effect, who or what bears it, and the period considered.

An outcome after a decision is not automatically caused by that decision. Assess alternative causes and uncertainty before assigning responsibility for the consequence.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

circumstances relevant to interpreting or judging an object or action

Context

Context is the set of circumstances that helps determine what an object, statement, action, or result means and how it should be judged.

Read explanation

For a quality judgment, relevant context can include the intended use, users, setting, applicable requirements, and constraints. State the circumstances that could change the interpretation.

Context does not excuse an unsupported claim. It tells the reader which conditions the claim applies to and where its conclusion may no longer hold.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

recurring evidence-guided improvement work

Continuous improvement

Continuous improvement is recurring, organized work to improve how a process or system meets its aims.

Read explanation

It starts with a stated need and a proposed change, then checks what actually happened. An unsuccessful test can still produce useful learning.

Effective changes should become part of routine practice and remain open to later review. Continuous does not mean that performance rises without interruption or that every change is beneficial.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

time-ordered process variation chart

Control chart

A control chart is a time-ordered display of a process measure with a centerline and statistically derived control limits, used to detect signals of process change.

Read explanation

Points beyond a limit or certain nonrandom patterns can prompt investigation of a special cause. Chart type, sampling plan, and signal rules must fit the data and process.

Control limits describe expected process behavior under the chart model. They are different from specification limits, which state what an output is required to meet.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

documented arrangements for monitoring and responding to process variation

Control plan

A control plan is a document specifying how a defined process and its important characteristics will be monitored and what action follows when results depart from stated conditions.

Read explanation

It commonly names what to check, the method and frequency, the acceptable limits, who records results, and what to do when a limit is breached.

A chart or target alone is not a complete control plan: people also need responsibility and response instructions for ongoing operation.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

addressing causes of a detected nonconformity

Corrective action

Corrective action addresses the cause of a detected nonconformity or other unwanted situation so it does not recur.

Read explanation

Repairing or replacing one affected item corrects that case. Corrective action changes the condition that produced it; the two may both be needed.

The action should follow an investigation proportionate to the problem. Its effectiveness must be checked against later evidence rather than assumed from implementation alone.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

CAPA approach to actual and potential quality problems

Corrective and preventive action

Corrective and preventive action, or CAPA, is a structured approach to addressing actual and potential quality problems through the actions appropriate to each case.

Read explanation

It distinguishes correcting a detected case, addressing a cause of recurrence, and addressing a cause of a potential problem. These are different tasks; a case does not automatically require every kind of action.

A CAPA record can connect investigation, planned actions, implementation, and an effectiveness check. Its purpose is to show that the response fits the problem and that the intended change occurred.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

technical-interaction roof in a House of Quality

Correlation matrix

In a House of Quality, the correlation matrix is a roof-shaped grid for assessing how proposed technical characteristics interact with one another.

Read explanation

A positive mark suggests that changes may reinforce one another; a negative mark flags a possible conflict or trade-off. The meaning of each mark depends on the method and evidence used.

In this use, “correlation” need not mean a calculated statistical correlation coefficient. The roof records design judgments unless a separate analysis establishes measured relationships.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

costs attributable to defects and other quality failures

Cost of poor quality (COPQ)

Cost of poor quality (COPQ) is the estimated cost attributable to failures to meet relevant quality requirements.

Read explanation

It can include scrap and rework found before delivery, and returns, warranty work, complaints, or other consequences found afterward. Some costs are difficult to observe or assign.

An estimate needs a stated accounting boundary, time period, and basis for attributing costs. Prevention and appraisal costs are often reported separately as costs of achieving quality; naming the convention avoids misleading comparisons.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

costs of achieving and failing to achieve quality

Cost of quality

Cost of quality is the set of costs incurred to prevent, assess, and deal with failures to meet quality requirements.

Read explanation

A common account separates prevention and appraisal costs from internal failure costs found before delivery and external failure costs found afterward. State which costs are included and the period measured.

The accounting total can reveal where improvement may save resources, but it does not capture every harm or benefit of quality. A low recorded cost is not, by itself, evidence that quality is high.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

evaluative basis or consideration

Criterion

A criterion is a consideration used as a basis for a judgment.

Read explanation

In evaluation, it identifies something that matters about the subject being assessed, such as accessibility, safety, relevance, or timeliness.

The evaluator must still establish why that consideration matters and what evidence can support a judgment about it.

The subject being assessed is an evaluand; criteria provide grounds for judging its merit or worth.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

hazard-control boundary at a food-safety HACCP critical control point

Critical limit

In a food-safety HACCP plan, a critical limit is a stated boundary for a monitored parameter at a critical control point, chosen to control a specified hazard.

Read explanation

The limit identifies the parameter, direction, and value or observable condition. It needs a justified basis for the food, process, and hazard.

Monitoring checks whether the limit is met. A deviation triggers the plan’s corrective-action procedure; it does not by itself establish that every affected product is unsafe. A critical limit differs from an operating target set only for efficiency or product quality.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

assessable characteristic consequential to a customer need

Critical to quality (CTQ)

A critical-to-quality (CTQ) characteristic is an assessable feature of a product, service, or process whose performance matters materially to meeting a customer need.

Read explanation

CTQ work translates a broad need into characteristics and requirements that can be checked. The link between the need and the characteristic should be made explicit.

Calling a characteristic “critical” does not make every available measure relevant. Its priority and acceptable performance must be justified for the particular customer and use.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

quality-management orientation toward customer needs and requirements

Customer focus

In quality management, customer focus is an organizational commitment to understand customer needs and use them to shape outputs, decisions, and improvement.

Read explanation

It includes learning what customers need, meeting relevant requirements, and acting on evidence about their experience.

Customer focus does not mean adopting every expressed preference without weighing other requirements and affected parties.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

direct customer conversation to elicit needs and experience

Customer interview

A customer interview is a direct conversation used to learn a customer’s needs, experiences, expectations, or judgments relevant to a product or service.

Read explanation

An interviewer can ask planned questions and follow up on unexpected answers. The study should identify which customers were approached, the setting, and the purpose of the questions.

An interview provides evidence of what that person reports; general claims about all customers require an appropriate sampling and analysis basis.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

customer-side need or expectation in QFD

Customer requirement

In QFD, a customer requirement is a stated or investigated need or expectation that a proposed product or service should satisfy for a specified customer and use.

Read explanation

A broad request may need to be clarified into terms that can guide design and later assessment. An inferred need should be labeled and checked rather than presented as something the customer explicitly said.

The requirement describes the customer-side aim. A technical characteristic is a proposed design response, and a technical target specifies a desired level for that response.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

customer appraisal of an offering or experience

Customer satisfaction

Customer satisfaction is a customer’s appraisal of how well a product, service, or experience meets relevant needs and expectations.

Read explanation

It is tied to a particular customer, offering, and time. Expectations and perceptions can change, so a satisfaction result should state who was asked and what experience they judged.

Satisfaction is informative about the customer’s view, but it does not by itself establish conformity to every requirement or the absence of harm to others.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

suitability of data for a specified use

Data quality

Data quality is how well data are suited to a specified use and its requirements.

Read explanation

Aspects such as accuracy, completeness, consistency, and currency can matter, but their relevance depends on the data, question, and setting. A useful assessment says which aspect was checked and how.

Data that support one decision may be inadequate for another. A quality dimension names a concern; a metric or check specifies how evidence about that concern is obtained.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

choice of a course of action

Decision

A decision is a choice among possible courses of action, including whether to act, defer, or seek more information.

Read explanation

A defensible decision identifies the options, the aim, the relevant evidence, and the likely consequences and uncertainties. A judgment or evaluation can inform the choice without uniquely determining it.

The decision is the choice made; its supporting inference, criteria, and evidence are distinct parts of its justification.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

appraisal of a choice under uncertainty

Decision quality

Decision quality is how well a choice is made in light of its aims, available alternatives, relevant information, uncertainty, and the values at stake.

Read explanation

A sound appraisal asks what could reasonably be known and considered when the choice was made. It should identify whose objectives and consequences matter.

The result of a decision is relevant for learning, but it is not the same as decision quality: chance or later events can turn a well-made choice into a poor outcome, or the reverse.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

stated procedure turning evidence and criteria into a disposition

Decision rule

A decision rule specifies how stated evidence and criteria lead to a selection, classification, acceptance, rejection, or other disposition.

Read explanation

A rule may use cutoffs, an order of priorities, a weighted combination, or a treatment of uncertainty. The relevant inputs and conditions must be clear enough to apply it consistently.

Different defensible rules can produce different outcomes from the same evidence. The rule and its authority should therefore be stated before treating a result as a decision.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

nonconformity related to intended or specified use

Defect

In quality management, a defect is a nonconformity related to an intended or specified use.

Read explanation

The link to use matters: a departure may violate a requirement without being shown to affect the use in question. Identify the object, the unmet requirement, and the use before applying the narrower label.

A defect may be found through inspection, testing, or use. Finding one calls for a response to the affected case; preventing recurrence may also require corrective action.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

structured quality-focused design of new or substantially redesigned offerings

Design for Six Sigma (DFSS)

Design for Six Sigma (DFSS) is a family of structured approaches for designing a new or substantially redesigned product, service, or process to meet specified customer and performance requirements.

Read explanation

It brings customer needs into design decisions, develops and tests candidate solutions, and considers how the design will perform under expected variation. Different DFSS roadmaps use different phases and tools.

DFSS addresses quality while designing; improving an established process without substantial redesign is usually framed as a different Six Sigma task.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

planned comparison of factor effects on a response

Design of experiments (DOE)

Design of experiments (DOE) is the planned variation of input factors to learn how they affect a specified response.

Read explanation

An experimental design states the factors, their settings, the response to observe, and how runs will be compared. It can reveal interactions that a one-factor-at-a-time check may miss.

The design must fit the question and available resources. A pattern found in the tested conditions needs further support before it is assumed to hold under different conditions.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

structured examination of a design against requirements

Design review

A design review is a planned, documented examination of a design to judge whether it can meet relevant requirements.

Read explanation

It considers the design as a whole against its intended functions and requirements, bringing unresolved questions into view before a decision to proceed or revise.

Review is an examination and judgment. It should not be confused with a test result or with proof that a finished design will succeed in every use.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

FMEA estimate of discovering a failure or its cause

Detection

In failure mode and effects analysis, detection is the estimated likelihood that a specified failure or its cause will be discovered at a stated stage.

Read explanation

The estimate depends on what is being sought and which controls or observers could find it. Some schemes assess discovery before delivery; others assess whether a customer will notice the effect.

A detection rating is separate from the failure’s likelihood and severity. State the scale and its direction: on many FMEA scales, a higher detection number means the problem is harder to find.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

aspect in an evaluative framework

Dimension

In an evaluative framework, a dimension is a distinguishable aspect used to organize what is described or judged.

Read explanation

For data quality, accuracy and completeness can be treated as different dimensions. Which dimensions matter depends on the subject and purpose.

A dimension names an aspect; an indicator or measure supplies information about it. This use differs from mathematical dimensions such as length or time.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

evaluation of educational opportunities, processes and outcomes

Education quality

Education quality is how well educational opportunities, processes, and outcomes serve justified learning aims and the needs of learners in a stated context.

Read explanation

A judgment may consider what learners gain, the teaching and support they receive, access, fairness, and the resources that make learning possible. Which considerations matter depends on the learners, institution, and purpose of the evaluation.

A test score or institutional rating is evidence about selected aspects, not a complete judgment of education quality by itself.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

conditions of an environment relative to ecological or human purposes

Environmental quality

Environmental quality is how favorable the conditions of a specified environment are for relevant ecological or human purposes.

Read explanation

A judgment identifies the place, time, affected living systems or people, and criteria. Air, water, and soil conditions can each matter, but no single indicator describes the whole environment.

An acceptable limit for one pollutant answers a narrower question than whether the environment is good overall. The assessment also depends on the chosen reference condition, exposure, and intended use.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

fair access, treatment and outcomes across groups

Equity

Equity concerns whether people or groups have fair access, treatment, and opportunities in light of relevant needs and barriers.

Read explanation

Identical treatment can leave unjustified differences in opportunity intact when starting conditions differ. An equity claim should say which population, benefit, barrier, and standard of fairness are at issue.

In health care, equity includes avoiding differences in care quality attributable to personal or social characteristics that should not determine care. In education, it can concern persistent barriers to access and attainment.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

evaluation terminology

Evaluand

An evaluand is the specific thing being evaluated.

Read explanation

Depending on the evaluation, it may be a program, policy, product, service, organization, person’s performance, or other subject.

The term names a role in an evaluation: it does not, by itself, say whether that thing is good, effective, or valuable.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

reasoned appraisal of a subject

Evaluation

Evaluation is the process of appraising a specified subject against relevant grounds to reach a conclusion about its merit, worth, significance, or other value.

Read explanation

It brings information together with criteria or values and interprets the result for a stated purpose. The word can also name the resulting appraisal.

An evaluation may draw on assessments, measurements, or testimony; none of those alone settles the value judgment. What counts as adequate evidence depends on the question and setting.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

cross-domain claim-bearing evidence

Evidence

Evidence is information or material brought to bear on a claim: for example, observations, records, testimony, or a physical object.

Read explanation

What it establishes depends on which claim is at issue and how the material was obtained and interpreted.

Evidence can support a claim, count against it, or be too weak to settle it.

There is no single evidence threshold that applies across research, quality audits, and courts.

In quality management, objective evidence is data that supports the existence or truth of something. In U.S. federal evidence law, relevance depends on whether material makes a consequential fact more or less probable.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

warrant of a body of evidence for a specified conclusion

Evidence quality

Evidence quality is the degree of confidence a body of evidence warrants for a specified question or conclusion.

Read explanation

Relevant concerns include how the evidence was produced, whether findings agree, how directly they address the question, and how precise they are. A strong individual study does not automatically make the whole body of evidence strong.

The quality of evidence and the strength of a recommendation are separate judgments: a decision also depends on benefits, harms, values, and context.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

cross-domain elevated appraisal

Excellence

Excellence is exceptionally strong merit or performance in a relevant respect, judged by standards appropriate to the activity, object, or person.

Read explanation

Calling something excellent therefore requires more than saying that it passed a minimum requirement: the judgment must identify what it excels at and provide a sound basis for the elevated appraisal.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

cost arising from defects discovered during or after delivery

External failure cost

An external failure cost is a cost arising from a defect or nonconformity found during or after delivery of a product or service.

Read explanation

Examples include returns, warranty work, complaint handling, and repairs after the customer receives the offering. The cost belongs to the failure category because the problem has occurred.

A cost estimate should state what delivery boundary and consequences it includes. Possible losses of trust or future sales may be relevant, but their monetary attribution needs separate support.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

input or condition studied in a designed experiment

Factor

In a designed experiment, a factor is an input or condition studied for its effect on a specified response.

Read explanation

The experiment uses particular values, called levels, of the factor and records the response at those levels. The design should say which levels are studied.

A factor is distinct from the response being measured. Calling a variable a factor does not by itself establish that it causes a change.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

costs arising from defects or nonconformity

Failure cost

A failure cost is a cost that arises because a product, service, or process fails to meet a relevant quality requirement.

Read explanation

Internal failure costs arise before delivery, such as scrap or rework. External failure costs arise during or after delivery, such as returns, warranty work, or complaint handling.

The estimate depends on the accounting boundary: some consequences, including lost trust or delayed work, can be difficult to attribute or price. Prevention and appraisal costs belong to different categories.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

consequence of a specified failure mode for a function or affected party

Failure effect

A failure effect is a consequence that may result when a specified failure mode occurs.

Read explanation

Describe the effect at a stated level, such as the affected component, system, service, or user. One failure mode may have several effects at different levels.

The effect answers what happens because of the failure; the failure mode describes how function is lost, and a cause explains why that mode arose.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

specified way an item or process fails

Failure mode

A failure mode is a specified way in which an item, process, or system fails to perform an intended function.

Read explanation

Depending on the analysis, it may describe a visible symptom, a functional failure, or a physical mechanism. State the intended level: what one analysis calls a mode may be treated as a cause or effect in another.

The same item can have several failure modes, so a useful description identifies the affected function and the particular manner of failure.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

prospective failure analysis

Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)

Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) is a structured, prospective examination of how a product, process, or system might fail, what could cause each failure, and what its effects would be.

Read explanation

The analysis helps a team choose actions that reduce the likelihood or consequences of significant failures. It should be revisited when the design, process, or evidence changes.

Severity, occurrence, and detectability ratings may help organize priorities, but a combined score does not replace judgment about context, especially when consequences differ sharply.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

justifiability of treatment, process or outcome

Fairness

Fairness is the quality of treatment, a decision process, or an outcome being justifiable under standards relevant to those affected.

Read explanation

In a decision, the procedure and the result can be judged separately. A fair procedure does not guarantee that everyone will regard the outcome as fair, and a favorable outcome does not establish that the procedure was fair.

People’s perceptions of fairness matter for trust and response to a decision. Those perceptions are evidence of reception, not conclusive proof that the decision was justified.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

top-down logical analysis of a specified undesired event

Fault tree analysis (FTA)

Fault tree analysis (FTA) is a top-down method for showing how combinations of lower-level events could produce a specified unwanted event.

Read explanation

A tree starts with that top event and connects contributing events through logical gates, such as “and” or “or.” The structure helps identify combinations worth investigating or controlling.

The tree can support qualitative analysis; estimating event likelihoods also requires defensible event data and model assumptions. A diagram alone does not establish that its listed events are the actual causes of an occurrence.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

share of units passing a defined process on their first attempt

First-pass yield

First-pass yield is the proportion of units entering a specified process step that meet its acceptance criteria on the first attempt, without rework or retesting.

Read explanation

Count first-pass conforming units over all eligible units entering the step. State the step, unit, population, and time period so the result can be compared meaningfully.

A unit repaired and accepted later may contribute to final output, but it did not pass on the first attempt. For a multistep flow, a separate rolled yield addresses passing every step without rework.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

suitability for a stated aim

Fitness for purpose

Fitness for purpose is how suitable an object is to achieve a specified purpose under relevant conditions.

Read explanation

State the purpose, whose purpose it is, and the conditions that matter. A claim may concern an ordinary intended use or a specially declared one.

The phrase often overlaps with fitness for use. It does not prove that every requirement is met, and meeting listed requirements does not always establish fitness for the intended purpose.

CC0 1.0View versioned object

suitability in a user’s context

Fitness for use

Fitness for use is how suitable an object is for a user’s intended activity under the conditions in which it will be used.

Read explanation

The judgment depends on the user, task, setting, and consequences of failure. An object can meet its written specification yet be unsuitable for the intended use if the specification missed a relevant need.

Fitness for use and fitness for purpose overlap. This wording emphasizes the conditions of actual or intended use, without assigning the phrases mutually exclusive meanings.
